The market is pessimistic. For C$33 to be a fair price, Docebo would have to lose about a quarter of its recurring revenue every year. Its own reports point to about 9%. In retention terms, the price assumes 83% net revenue retention. Docebo reported 99%.

The idea, like you've never seen finance before

Think of a rental house. Normally you'd say: "It earns $2,000 a month, so it's worth about $400,000." That's working forward.

Working backwards: "It's selling for $250,000. What rent must buyers expect to get?" If the answer is much lower than the real rent, buyers are scared of something, or the house is a bargain.

We do exactly that with a stock. The share price is the selling price. Churn (customers cancelling) is the thing we solve for.

Words to know (copy these into your notebook)

ARR
Annual recurring revenue. The yearly value of all active subscriptions right now.Docebo: US$255.1M on 30 Jun 2026.
Churn
The share of last year's ARR that cancelled or downgraded.Lose $9 of every $100 → 9% churn.
Expansion
Extra ARR from existing customers buying more (more seats, add-ons).Our assumption: 8% a year.
Net revenue retention (NRR)
Of every $100 customers paid last year, how much they pay this year. Equals 100% − churn + expansion. Above 100% is good.Docebo 2025: 99% (101% without Amazon's AWS, a big customer that left).
ARR bridge
Starting ARR + new + expansion − churn = ending ARR. Your SaaS model's customer roll-forward, in dollars.
EBITDA
Profit before interest, taxes and depreciation. A rough measure of operating profit.Docebo's last 12 months: 18.6% of revenue.
Free cash flow (FCF)
Cash from operations minus spending on equipment and software. The money actually left over.Last 12 months: US$48.7M.
Discount rate
How much you shrink future money each year, because $1 later is worth less than $1 now (risk, waiting). At 10%, $110 next year = $100 today.
Terminal value
One lump sum for all the years after Year 10: FCF in Year 11 ÷ (discount rate − long-run growth).Here it's 56% of the total value. That's normal, and also why small changes matter.
Enterprise value (EV)
What the whole business is worth: the sum of all discounted cash flows.
Equity value
EV + cash − debt. What belongs to shareholders. Divide by the number of shares to get the value per share.
DCF
Discounted cash flow. Forecast the cash, shrink it to today's dollars, add it up.
Reverse DCF
Fix the answer at today's price and solve for the assumption. That assumption is the market's hidden bet.
Goal Seek
Excel tool: "change this input until that output equals X." We use it to find the churn that gives C$33.22.
Sensitivity table
A grid of answers for many combinations of two inputs. Excel builds one with Data → What-If Analysis → Data Table.

Is the market right to be scared?

A reverse DCF doesn't prove the stock is cheap. It shows you what you'd have to believe. Reasons the market might be pricing in something bad:

  • AI disruption. Investors fear AI tools could make corporate training platforms cheaper or unnecessary. That would show up as higher churn and lower prices.
  • Big customers leaving. AWS (Amazon) was about 6% of ARR and is winding down, and a Dayforce partnership is ending too. Both hurt reported growth.
  • Growth slowed. ARR growth fell from about 18% in 2024 to about 9.5% now, although management says underlying growth is about 14%.
  • Stock-based pay. Adjusted EBITDA leaves out stock-based compensation, which is a real cost. That makes our DCF a bit generous.
  • Control and size. Intercap owns about 64%, so fewer shares trade freely. Smaller, controlled companies often trade at a discount.

Our lens also blames everything on churn. The market's worry might really be slower new sales or lower margins, and you can run the same reverse trick on those levers.

One-page write-up (draft, ready to edit)

What Docebo's stock price is betting on

The company. Docebo (TSX: DCBO) sells an AI-powered learning platform by subscription to businesses around the world. It has US$255M of annual recurring revenue growing about 10% a year, 79% gross margins, and US$49M of free cash flow over the last twelve months. The stock trades at C$33, about 2.5× ARR and 13× free cash flow.

The question. Instead of guessing what Docebo is worth, I asked what the current price assumes. I built a 10-year ARR model (starting ARR + new + expansion − churn), turned it into free cash flow using Docebo's own margins, discounted it at 10% with a 2.5% terminal growth rate, and solved for the churn rate that makes the model equal the market price.

The finding. The market price implies Docebo loses about 24.5% of its ARR every year, which equals a net revenue retention of about 83%. Docebo's reported retention of 99% implies about 9% churn. At reported churn, the model values the shares at about C$70.

Sensitivity. Across discount rates of 8–12%, today's price needs churn of roughly 20–30%. Even the harshest case at reported churn gives C$53 or more.

Conclusion. The market is far more pessimistic than Docebo's reported numbers, most likely because it fears AI will disrupt corporate learning and because big customers like AWS are leaving. If retention holds near 99% and margins keep expanding, the stock looks undervalued. The key thing to watch is net revenue retention in the next results: that's the number that decides who is right.

Interview answers to memorize

Your 30-second pitch

"I built a reverse DCF on Docebo at the customer level. Instead of forecasting a price, I solved for the churn rate the market price implies. At C$33, the market is pricing in about 25% annual ARR churn, while Docebo's 99% net retention implies about 9%. So either the market expects AI disruption to hit retention hard, or the stock is undervalued. I'd watch net revenue retention next quarter to find out."

"Walk me through a DCF."
Forecast free cash flow for about 10 years. Discount each year back to today using the discount rate. Add a terminal value for the years after that, also discounted. That gives enterprise value. Add cash and subtract debt for equity value, then divide by shares.
"Why a reverse DCF?"
Forecasts are guesses. A reverse DCF turns the price into one clear assumption I can check against real data, so I'm testing the market's expectation rather than defending my own.
"What's the weakest part of your model?"
The terminal value is over half the value, and it's very sensitive to the discount rate. Also, Docebo doesn't report gross churn, so I backed it out of net retention with an 8% expansion assumption, and adjusted EBITDA excludes stock-based pay.
"How would you pick the discount rate properly?"
Use WACC: the cost of equity (risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium) blended with the after-tax cost of debt, weighted by how much of each the company uses. For a small software company it usually lands around 9–12%, which is why the grid covers that range.
